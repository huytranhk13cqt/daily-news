### 🔭 Overview (Tổng)
Amazon Redshift's new RG instance family, announced on 02/07/2026, is the first Redshift generation built on AWS Graviton silicon and the first to fold the Redshift Spectrum data-lake compute back into the warehouse node itself. The stated result is up to 2.2x faster warehouse workloads, 2.4x faster Apache Iceberg queries, and 30% lower per-vCPU cost against the outgoing RA3 fleet. For any team running Redshift at scale, this is the biggest shift in the underlying execution engine since RA3 introduced managed storage in 2020, and it removes the per-TB scanning fee that pushed heavy Iceberg workloads onto Athena or third-party engines.

### 🔬 Details (Phân)
Under the covers, RG combines Graviton-optimised SIMD kernels for the vectorised executor, custom Nitro SSDs for local caching, and an integrated data-lake scan layer that replaces the offloaded Spectrum compute fleet. AWS also introduces a JIT Analyze feature that collects statistics from Parquet and Iceberg files at query time, cutting the "cold catalog" penalty that made federated Redshift queries unpredictable. Ingestion improves in the same order — TPC-DS load is quoted at 2x faster and TPC-H at 1.4x, driven by the smart-prefetch scheduler that co-issues catalog and file I/O.

Positioning matters because the data-lake numbers are the release's real story. On RA3, every terabyte scanned through Spectrum carried a $5 charge that made Iceberg-backed dashboards operationally awkward to budget; RG removes that line item and instead prices data-lake scans as part of the compute you already own. Together with partition and file-level pruning at the integrated scan layer, AWS quotes up to 4.2x better price-performance versus RA3, and cites Southwest Airlines reporting 50–60% faster mixed workloads and Tombola seeing 1.8x–2.2x faster read throughput. The catch is that migration is not automatic — RG is a new instance family that requires a resize, and workloads with heavy compiled-plan caches will need to warm up again after cutover.

### 🎯 Takeaway (Hợp)
If your Redshift bill is dominated by Spectrum scans or your Iceberg queries feel disproportionately expensive, put RG on the roadmap before the next quarterly review — the removal of the $5/TB line item alone is worth modelling against your current data-lake spend. Plan the resize during a low-traffic window, warm the compiled-plan cache with a canned benchmark set, and keep an RA3 slice as a fallback until you have a week of production latency data.

### 🔭 Overview (Tổng)
On 01/07/2026 AWS added a new domain-level "Observability" engine to Amazon OpenSearch Service that stores data in Apache Parquet, executes analytical queries through Apache DataFusion, and still exposes Lucene inverted indices for full-text search on the same records. AWS quotes up to 4x better price-performance and 70% lower storage cost versus the classic Lucene-only OpenSearch stack. The move is a direct response to the last two years of teams routing logs into ClickHouse, Loki or DuckDB-on-S3 rather than pay for Elasticsearch-style JSON indexing, and it lands with published benchmark numbers rather than the usual vague uplift claims.

### 🔬 Details (Phân)
On a 24.4-billion-document benchmark, the new engine reached 1.78M documents/second sustained ingestion — roughly 2.75x the 647K achieved by the classic Lucene configuration on the same hardware — while compressing raw JSON to 20% of its original size, a 52% storage saving versus default Lucene. On the query side, error-severity aggregations over a 15-minute window return in 717 ms (3.9x faster), volume aggregations in 252 ms (70x faster) and single-trace lookups in 394 ms. Under the hood, the columnar layout leans on DataFusion's vectorised operators, and the engine keeps Lucene indices only for the fields you actually search on, so cardinality-heavy dimensions no longer pay the JSON indexing tax.

The trade-offs are worth calling out. The engine is a domain-level flag chosen at creation time, so you cannot flip an existing cluster into it — you provision a new domain and migrate ingestion pipelines across. The DSL query API is not supported at all in this mode; you write in Piped Processing Language (PPL) or SQL, which will invalidate hand-written Kibana visualisations that rely on DSL bodies. Pricing is the standard instance-plus-storage model, with no per-domain premium, and it is generally available in twelve regions from launch across US, Canada, Europe and Asia Pacific.

### 🎯 Takeaway (Hợp)
If you are running an OpenSearch cluster whose bill is dominated by hot-tier log ingestion, spin up a small Observability-mode domain this sprint and shadow-index a single service's logs to compare storage and query cost end-to-end. Assume a rewrite of any DSL-heavy dashboards to PPL before you commit — and keep the classic domain live in parallel until you have benchmarked your slowest text-search queries against the new Lucene-plus-columnar hybrid.

## 3. ClickHouse Details How ClickStack Got 5x Faster Through Primary-Key and Index Redesign

### 🔭 Overview (Tổng)
On 02/07/2026 ClickHouse published a 5x latency reduction retrospective for ClickStack, its observability stack, walking through the exact schema and index changes that landed the win. The benchmarks were driven by a new in-house harness called ClickCannon at 640 MiB/s sustained ingestion — roughly 1.5 PB/month — which is honest scale rather than a marketing microbenchmark. Anyone running ClickHouse for logs or traces will find at least one of the four techniques directly applicable, and the post is the clearest public breakdown so far of how text indexes replace Bloom filters in real workloads.

### 🔬 Details (Phân)
The single biggest gain came from reordering the primary key from `(ServiceName, TimestampTime)` to `(toStartOfFiveMinutes(Timestamp), ServiceName, Timestamp)`. Five-minute bucketing preserves contiguous disk ranges and unlocks the `read_in_order` optimisation for Top-N queries — the shape 90% of dashboards actually run. On top of that, ClickHouse swapped Bloom filters on `TraceId` for text indexes (up to 22% faster on selective lookups) and moved the `Body` column from `tokenbf_v1` to a text index with the `splitByNonAlpha` tokeniser, giving deterministic matching semantics that eliminate a class of "why is my query slow today" incidents.

The map-attribute optimisation is the most transferable technique. ClickStack added alias columns that concatenate `key=value` pairs into a single searchable array, letting the query planner apply the new `direct_read` fast path and delivering up to 10x speedups on tag-filtered queries. The team also flipped on the `_block_number` and `_block_offset` virtual columns to short-circuit row lookups, and moved autocomplete and metadata discovery to pre-aggregated AggregatingMergeTree rollups so those small, chatty queries stop competing with the primary-key search plan. The caveat is that swapping a primary key on an active table requires a full rewrite window; the post gives no ready-made online migration recipe.

### 🎯 Takeaway (Hợp)
If you already run ClickHouse for logs or traces, audit your primary key against the five-minute-bucket-plus-service pattern this week — you can prototype the change on a shadow table before committing to a rewrite. Then piggyback the text-index swap and the alias-column trick during the same maintenance window; both are cheap to add and pay back the moment you land the primary-key rewrite.

### 💡 Example
```sql
-- ClickStack-style primary key: five-minute bucket first, then service, then event time.
-- Combined with a text index on TraceId and an alias column over tag map attributes.
CREATE TABLE otel_logs
(
    Timestamp           DateTime64(9),
    ServiceName         LowCardinality(String),
    TraceId             String,
    Body                String,
    Attributes          Map(String, String),
    -- Alias column that direct_read can scan as a single array.
    AttributesKV        Array(String) ALIAS arrayMap(
                            (k, v) -> concat(k, '=', v),
                            mapKeys(Attributes),
                            mapValues(Attributes)),
    INDEX idx_trace     TraceId TYPE text(tokenizer = 'default'),
    INDEX idx_body      Body    TYPE text(tokenizer = 'splitByNonAlpha')
)
ENGINE = MergeTree
ORDER BY (toStartOfFiveMinutes(Timestamp), ServiceName, Timestamp);
```

### 🔬 Details (Phân)

The Panda Sense Pro is the first ready-to-ship device that speaks both Home Assistant and Moonraker out of the box, and the sensor mix targets the exact pollutants that print farms create rather than the general household TVOC panel. The trade-off is that HCHO electrochemical cells have finite lifetimes and drift; expect a calibration or replacement cycle within about two years of continuous duty.

### 🔭 Overview (Tổng)
A weekend project spotted on 05/07/2026 emulates a Macintosh Quadra 610 — a 25 MHz 68040 machine running System 7 through Mac OS 8.1 — on Espressif's ESP32-P4 microcontroller, targeting the M5Stack Tab5 and the Waveshare ESP32-P4-WIFI6-Touch-LCD-10.1 kits. The emulator delivers roughly 2–3 MIPS of 68040 throughput and 24 FPS of video at 640x360, with 4 to 16 MB of virtualised RAM. It is a small piece of retro-computing news but a useful data point for anyone benchmarking the ESP32-P4's dual-core RISC-V for compute-heavy embedded workloads.

### 🔬 Details (Phân)
Technically, the project splits the workload across the ESP32-P4's two cores: one core handles CPU emulation with write-time dirty tracking, the other handles video, touch and I/O with tile-based rendering to keep only the changed screen regions in the DMA path. That partitioning is why the project can hit 24 FPS on the 5-inch 1280x720 Tab5 and the 10.1-inch 1280x800 Waveshare panel while sustaining single-digit-MIPS emulation of a chip that itself topped out around 60 MIPS in 1993. The source is on GitHub at `amcchord/M5Tab-Macintosh`, and the README notes it was authored with Anthropic Claude's assistance — a small signal about how much LLM-assisted work is now shipping in open-source embedded projects.

The context worth flagging is what this says about the ESP32-P4 platform. The P4 was pitched at HMI, edge AI and touch-heavy applications; the ability to emulate a 68040 in real time confirms Espressif's throughput claims outside of curated benchmarks. It also demonstrates a reusable pattern for other embedded emulation or streaming workloads on the P4: one core for the tight compute loop, the other core carrying display and I/O with dirty-region tracking. Beyond the novelty, the project is a good stress test for anyone evaluating the Tab5 or the Waveshare touch kit as a HMI platform.

### 🎯 Takeaway (Hợp)
If you are evaluating the ESP32-P4 for a touch-heavy HMI or an edge-AI panel, flash this project onto a Tab5 to see the dual-core split in a real workload rather than a benchmark harness — the tile-based rendering pattern is worth copying. The novelty is in the ceiling it exposes: the P4 is now legitimately fast enough for HMIs that used to require a Cortex-A class SoC.
