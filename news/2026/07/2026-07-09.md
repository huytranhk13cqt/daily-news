## 1. Amazon OpenSearch Service Ships Writable Warm Storage on OI2, Cuts Cost Up To 48% vs UltraWarm

### 🔭 Overview (Tổng)
AWS announced on 08/07/2026 that Amazon OpenSearch Service can now perform active writes, background merges and periodic refreshes directly on warm nodes, ending the long-standing constraint that made UltraWarm a read-only tier. The feature is delivered through a new OI2 instance family that treats Amazon S3 as durable storage for both hot and warm shards, so tier transitions become lightweight shard relocations rather than full physical migrations. For teams running large log and observability estates on OpenSearch the change collapses a familiar operational tax — the hours needed to promote data back to hot just to correct or enrich it.

### 🔬 Details (Phân)
The technical requirements are specific and worth noting before planning a rollout. Writable warm needs OpenSearch Service version 3.3 or later, data nodes on either OR2 (hot) or OI2 (warm) instance families, an OI2 SKU available in your region, and a minimum refresh interval of five seconds; the cold tier is not yet supported for writable operations. The workflow example in the post shows a hot-to-writable-warm transition where updates then execute directly in seconds instead of the roughly 130 minutes per 100 GB the legacy UltraWarm path required, because there is no round trip through hot for background merges or delete-by-query.

The cost story is grounded in Bharav Patel's worked example. A 3-year Reserved Instance on OI2 comes in around 48% cheaper than the equivalent UltraWarm plan, while a 1-year AURI plan drops from about $69,674 to $43,918 monthly, roughly 36% off. Additional savings compound with 31–52% RI discounts and about 22% under Compute Savings Plans. The architectural shift matters more than the pricing table though: because S3 is the durable substrate for OI2, warm and hot behave as tiers on the same storage plane rather than as separate compute-and-storage domains, which is how competing lakehouse search products like Elastic's Frozen tier and ClickHouse Cloud already look. The trade-offs are that historical corrective backfills now become in-place updates rather than migrations, which is an operationally cleaner story but also concentrates write load on warm nodes that were previously read-only capacity.

### 🎯 Takeaway (Hợp)
If you run OpenSearch clusters where late-arriving fields or PII redactions currently trigger UltraWarm re-promotion, plan an upgrade to 3.3 and test OI2 warm write throughput on a representative index before the next quarter's Reserved Instance renewal. Keep an eye on refresh cost — the 5-second minimum is fine for logs but you may need to relax it further to stop small-shard write amplification eating the pricing win.

### 🔭 Overview (Tổng)
Cloudflare Research published on 08/07/2026 an experiment called Meerkat, authored by James Larisch, Bob Halley and João Pedro Leite, that acts as a global consensus log for the company's control plane. Meerkat implements QuePaxa, the leaderless consensus algorithm published by EPFL in 2023, and Cloudflare frames the deployment as the first industrial run of QuePaxa at global scale. For engineers building metadata services, leasing systems and transactional key-value stores across geographically distributed data centres, the post is a rare peek at how a hyperscaler reasons about the operational shortcomings of Raft.

### 🔬 Details (Phân)
The concrete pitch is that QuePaxa removes a class of failure Raft is notorious for. Any replica can drive consensus, so a "leader" is only an optimisation rather than a required role; concurrent proposals from different replicas do not interfere destructively; and progress does not depend on timeouts, so partial network flakiness does not stall the log the way Raft's leader-election path does. Cloudflare quotes the EPFL paper's benchmark of roughly 10x higher throughput than Raft under adverse network conditions, and its proof-of-concept clusters of 50 replicas ran with no error-rate increase despite constant leader failures being injected. Round-trip counts land at 1 for leader-driven proposals and 3 or more for leaderless paths, which lines up with the standard Paxos family cost model.

The interesting shape for a data engineer is the consistency layer. Meerkat guarantees linearizability — every read after a write sees that write — and the key-value store on top of it is serializable, which the post pushes as the right substrate for placement and leadership data across 330+ Cloudflare data centres. The operational advice is worth reading: batch small writes into single proposals (they cite 10 writes in 10 ms), use local-replica stale reads when the workload can tolerate them, and colocate a majority of replicas within a low-latency region to keep the decision path off the transpacific link. The caveats are honest — it is a research preview, not GA, and consensus latency is still bounded by the majority round-trip, so global writes will pay tens to hundreds of milliseconds regardless of algorithm smarts.

### 🎯 Takeaway (Hợp)
If you are still running Raft-backed metadata stores like etcd or Consul on wide-area network paths and hitting leader-flap incidents, keep an eye on the Meerkat public artefact and the QuePaxa reference implementation — they may become the honest alternative once battle-tested. In the meantime, adopt the batching and stale-read tactics from the post; they are portable to your existing Raft cluster today.

The framework converts real merged pull requests into well-specified coding challenges with associated test suites for pass/fail validation, spanning more than ten languages including Python, Go, TypeScript, Scala, Rust and Java plus configuration systems like Bazel and Protobuf. ### 🔬 Details (Phân)
The evaluation matrix runs frontier models across harnesses. Four headline findings emerge: models cluster into three clear performance tiers so mid-tier options are cost-effective for routine tasks; open models remain viable, with GLM 5.2 matching Opus quality at $1.28 per task versus $1.94 per task on Opus; per-token pricing is misleading because per-task cost varies by up to 2x due to reasoning-token and turn-count differences; and harness choice materially changes cost, with the same model producing roughly 2x cost variance across Claude Code vs Codex vs Pi.

The methodology angle is the durable takeaway. Building a benchmark from your own merged PRs — with the existing test suite as the oracle — gives you a graded rubric that is far more predictive of production usefulness than any leaderboard, and the post is explicit that the largest gains from switching models often come from re-tuning the harness rather than upgrading the SKU. It also positions Databricks against Cursor, GitHub Copilot Workspaces and Codex CLI, which all publish their own preferences rather than agnostic benchmarks. The trade-off is transparency: the raw task set is not open-sourced, so replication is limited to the shape of the methodology rather than the numbers themselves.

### 🎯 Takeaway (Hợp)

### 🔭 Overview (Tổng)
Home Assistant announced on 07/07/2026 that the Companion app will stop supporting iOS 15, watchOS 8 and macOS 11 starting with version 2026.8.0. The final compatible release for the affected platforms is 2026.7.1, and the new minimum floors are iOS 16.4, watchOS 9 and macOS 12. The change is small in isolation but a useful marker for anyone maintaining prosumer or SMB smart-home rollouts, since Home Assistant's Companion app is the primary control surface for many of them and OS-version splits reach right through to the phones sitting on their users' bedside tables.

### 🔬 Details (Phân)
The rationale in the announcement is a straight modernisation argument. Apple's developer tooling will officially stop supporting watchOS 8 and macOS 11 in September, and Home Assistant reports that less than 1% of Companion installs still run those OS versions, which means the maintenance cost of API polyfills and legacy build targets is no longer justified. The concrete gains the team names are Apple Watch complications, more advanced widgets and general UX, stability and performance headroom that older SDK boundaries prevented shipping cleanly. Users on legacy hardware retain browser access to the Home Assistant frontend, so the smart-home experience is not gone, but the native features that rely on the app disappear on those devices.

The broader context is worth pointing to for integrators. Home Assistant leans heavily on iOS-specific plumbing for actionable notifications, HomeKit bridge, geofencing and Apple Watch mini-dashboards, and dropping older SDK targets typically enables the maintainers to move to newer Swift concurrency, WidgetKit updates and Focus-mode integrations without maintaining twin implementations. The trade-off worth flagging is that fleets with a mix of iPad 2018 units running iPadOS 15 or old Watch Series 3 devices will now see the last-of-line 2026.7.1 as their permanent build, and any user-facing dashboard integration should assume no further security fixes on that path.
