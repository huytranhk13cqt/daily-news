## 1. EU Cyber Resilience Act Article 14 Reporting Duty Turns On September 11

| Field | Value |

### 🔭 Overview (Tổng)
The European Union's Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) enters into force in full on 11 December 2027, but its most operationally intrusive clause — Article 14, the incident- and vulnerability-reporting duty — starts to bite on 11 September 2026, three days after this digest. Every manufacturer of a "product with digital elements" placed on the EU market must, from that date, notify the designated national CSIRT and ENISA within 24 hours of becoming aware of an actively-exploited vulnerability or a severe incident affecting one of their products. The obligation is unusual among CRA duties in that it also covers legacy products already shipped before 11 December 2027, meaning ten-year-old firmware in a warehouse gateway is in scope the moment a live exploit surfaces. For IoT, home-automation, industrial-controls, and any embedded product vendor with EU customers, the compliance clock is now measured in days, not months.

### 🔬 Details (Phân)
The reporting workflow is a three-step cascade set out in Article 14(1)-(3). An "early warning" is due within 24 hours of the manufacturer becoming aware; it can be a short factual note ("we have observed active exploitation of vulnerability X in product Y") without a fix. A fuller notification, including a preliminary assessment of severity and any mitigation guidance for users, is due within 72 hours. A final report — root cause, corrective measures, and any indicators of compromise — is due within 14 days for actively-exploited vulnerabilities and within one month for severe incidents. Reports go into a single reporting platform coordinated by ENISA and the national CSIRTs, and the same submission satisfies both destinations. Manufacturers must also proactively inform affected users, proportionate to the risk, and disclose more broadly once a fix or mitigation is available.

The scope is broader than most vendors have modelled. Article 14 applies to any hardware or software with a data connection, whether it is a consumer smart bulb, an industrial LoRa gateway, a Linux distribution shipped commercially, or an SDK sold to another vendor. The exemption list is narrow — open-source projects distributed non-commercially remain outside the perimeter, but a company that ships an OSS component as part of a paid product does not inherit that exemption. Fines under the CRA can reach the higher of €15 million or 2.5% of global annual turnover for the reporting breaches themselves; the essential-requirements breaches (which do not turn on until 2027) carry higher ceilings.

The practical consequence is that any IoT product vendor with EU customers needs, this week, three things in place: (1) a monitored intake channel where vulnerabilities and incidents are triaged, timestamped, and escalated on a 24-hour clock; (2) a pre-registered reporting contact with the national CSIRT and an ENISA account on the single platform; (3) a bilingual template for the early-warning and 72-hour notifications so nobody drafts them from scratch under time pressure. Vendors already covered by NIS2 will recognise most of the structure, but CRA's product-level scope is wider and its "actively-exploited" trigger is stricter than NIS2's "significant incident" threshold.

### 🎯 Takeaway (Hợp)
Between now and 11 September, publish an internal one-pager naming the 24-hour on-call rota, the CSIRT/ENISA submission path, and the template early-warning language your team will use — every vendor selling connected products into the EU needs this before the deadline lands, not after the first exploit. Nếu bạn quản lý danh mục thiết bị IoT bán vào EU, đây là hạn chót cứng đầu tiên của CRA và có hiệu lực với cả sản phẩm đã bán trước 2027.

```yaml
  covers_legacy_products: true
```

That means chargeback and quota enforcement happen through the same tooling a data engineering team already runs for Genie Agents and Model Serving. The Astra model itself is positioned for structured document processing, coding, agentic search, multimodal inputs, and long-context workloads; on the same September batch Databricks also enabled Google Gemini 3.8 Flash (Sep 2) and Anthropic Claude Fable 5.1 (Sep 1) via the same path, alongside a Genie One beta that can now hit the public web and cite external sources in its answers.

## 4. Snowflake Promotes External Lineage and Restricted Agent Sessions to GA

### 🔭 Overview (Tổng)
Snowflake shipped three governance-shaped features across 3-4 September 2026: External Lineage reached general availability, Restricted Session Scope for Agents reached general availability, and the Prompt Injection Guardrails feature expanded into AWS_EU, AWS_JP, and AWS_APJ regions. Read together, the batch is Snowflake catching up to the reality that most warehouse queries in 2026 come from agents, that those agents call third-party systems, and that a governance model built for humans-only workloads leaks in three predictable places — cross-system lineage, over-broad sessions, and prompt manipulation. The three items are each modest on their own; the interesting story is that Snowflake is putting them behind the GA line together.

### 🔬 Details (Phân)
External Lineage extends Snowflake's existing column-level lineage graph past the Snowflake boundary. Prior to GA, lineage stopped at the edge of the Snowflake account — a Fivetran job that fed a table showed up as an opaque "external" source; a reverse-ETL pipe that pulled from a view was invisible. External Lineage lets partners and customers register upstream and downstream systems (identifiers, run metadata, column mappings) so a data engineer can trace a KPI in a downstream Tableau workbook back through Snowflake, back through the source ELT job, and back to a producing service. It is the same category of feature as OpenLineage / Marquez, but rendered as a first-class object inside the Snowflake account rather than as an external metadata store.

Restricted Session Scope for Agents is a narrower fix aimed directly at the "agent-as-user" pattern. When an agent authenticates to Snowflake, the default has been that it inherits the full role hierarchy of its principal. Restricted Session Scope lets a caller reduce the effective privileges for that session to a named subset — e.g. read-only against a single schema — for the duration of a single agent invocation, and the reduction is enforced by the query engine, not by the agent's own instructions. That closes the class of failures where a prompt injection tricks the agent into running `DROP TABLE`; even if the agent tries, the session lacks the privilege.

The Prompt Injection Guardrails expansion is the least novel of the three but the one that changes deployability. The Guardrails feature applies pattern-based detection to prompts crossing into Snowflake Cortex-hosted models; general availability in the US came earlier in the year, and the September 4 update rolls the same policy engine out to the AWS Europe, Japan, and Asia-Pacific-Jakarta regions, which unblocks compliance-driven customers who cannot route prompts through US regions. Together with External Lineage's cross-system trace and Restricted Session Scope's engine-enforced privilege reduction, Snowflake now has a defensible three-part story for agent governance that mirrors the "least privilege + observability + input filtering" playbook applied to human operators.

### 🎯 Takeaway (Hợp)
If you already run Cortex agents against Snowflake data, spend a sprint moving them onto Restricted Session Scope before the next threat-model review; External Lineage is where to invest if a downstream metric owner has ever asked "where did this number actually come from?". Nếu bạn ở EU/APAC và bị chặn bởi Guardrails-only-US, kiểm tra ngay khu vực AWS_EU / AWS_JP để bật lại đường agent đã dừng.

### 💡 Example
```sql
-- Restrict an agent's session to a read-only subset of Sales analytics for the
-- duration of one invocation. The engine enforces the drop; the agent cannot
-- climb back up its role hierarchy even if a prompt tries to make it.

BEGIN;
CALL SYSTEM$SET_SESSION_SCOPE(
  scope => OBJECT_CONSTRUCT(
    'roles',     ARRAY_CONSTRUCT('AGENT_SALES_READONLY'),
    'databases', ARRAY_CONSTRUCT('ANALYTICS'),
    'schemas',   ARRAY_CONSTRUCT('ANALYTICS.SALES'),
    'writes',    'DENY'
  )
);

-- The agent runs its planned SQL here; DROP / DELETE / MERGE will be denied
-- by the engine even if the agent constructs them.
COMMIT;
```

### 🔭 Overview (Tổng)
YuzukiHD published the YuzukiNeko board on 8 September 2026, an Allwinner F101 RISC-V single-board computer that fits a Raspberry Pi Pico footprint (roughly 51 × 21 mm) and runs mainline Linux 7.2. The novelty is not the SoC — Allwinner F101 is a modest 1.008 GHz single-core T-Head XuanTie C907 with 8 or 16 MB PSRAM — but the packaging: a Pico-shaped board that boots a real Linux userspace and drives an LVDS or MIPI-DSI panel up to 1280×800, at a target price under US$10. Schematics, EasyEDA PCB, Gerbers, BoM, and STEP file are on GitHub. For anyone who has been building HMI panels on Pico-shaped RP2040 modules and hitting the ceiling of a Cortex-M project, this is a drop-in-form-factor Linux upgrade path.

### 🔬 Details (Phân)
The SoC is Allwinner's low-cost RISC-V part: one T-Head XuanTie C907 core at up to 1.008 GHz with 32 KB I-cache and 16 KB D-cache, paired with either 8 MB or 16 MB of on-package PSRAM clocked at 252 MHz. Persistent storage is a 128 Mbit SPI NOR flash for the bootloader and a compact rootfs, plus a microSD slot for anything larger. The display side is where the F101 earns its keep in HMI use cases: it supports RGB888 up to 1280×720 at 60 fps, single-link LVDS up to 1366×768 at 60 fps, and 4-lane MIPI DSI up to 1280×800 at 60 fps. There are also five pads for analog speaker output plus four extra GPIOs beyond the two 20-pin headers that mirror the Raspberry Pi Pico pinout.

Software support is the story. Mainline Linux 7.2 runs on the F101 today, and YuzukiHD lists Zephyr RTOS as an alternative for bare-metal workloads that do not need the Linux surface. That is a materially different offer from the Pi Pico family (RP2040/RP2350), which is a Cortex-M microcontroller with no MMU and cannot boot mainline Linux; and from the ESP32-S3, which likewise does not run Linux. The closest existing product is the Milk-V Duo (Sophgo SG2000, dual RISC-V + tiny Cortex-A) — the YuzukiNeko sits below it on price and above it on display capability. There is no on-board wireless, so any BLE or Wi-Fi use case still needs a companion module.

The full open-hardware release — PDF schematic, EasyEDA project, Gerbers, 3D STEP, BoM — is on the YuzukiHD GitHub organisation, which makes the board interesting even before it ships: any team building an HMI product on a Pico footprint can pull the reference design straight into their own PCB. Pricing is not confirmed but the raw F101 SoC lists at about US$2.70, so a sub-US$10 finished board is plausible. Availability is "not yet for sale" as of 8 September.

### 🎯 Takeaway (Hợp)
If you have a fielded Raspberry Pi Pico project that has outgrown the Cortex-M0+ envelope but still has to live inside a 51 × 21 mm slot, put the YuzukiNeko on the "watch this ship" list and pull the schematic today for your next revision.
