### 🔭 Overview (Tổng)
On 22 October 2026 Sarah Chieng published a Cerebras engineering post describing how the company serves Kimi K3, an open-source model with roughly three trillion parameters, on a pipeline of 59 WSE-3T wafers. The piece hit the Hacker News front page on 25 October, which is why it is worth surfacing today for data platform teams: it is the first public, concrete description of how Cerebras composes multiple wafer-scale chips into a single inference service and what that costs in silicon per model. The headline number — "1000–2000+ tokens per second" — is less interesting on its own than the shape of the deployment, which is a pure pipeline rather than tensor- or data-parallel.

### 🔬 Details (Phân)
The physical substrate is the WSE-3T wafer with 44 GB of on-chip memory. At 4-bit weight quantization each K3 layer needs about 16 GB, so each dense layer takes one wafer (leaving room for its KV cache) and the thinner linear-attention layers pack two per wafer. The 24 dense layers and 69 linear-attention layers map to 24 and 35 wafers respectively, which gives the quoted total of 59 wafers for one serving replica. Weights stay pinned to the wafers that hold them; only activations travel between wafers, over RDMA-over-Ethernet links that the post times at roughly 2 µs per hop. That timing is the number that lets a pipeline make sense: a 59-stage pipeline with ~120 µs of pure hop cost would be unusable if each hop added hundreds of microseconds, and the design falls back to pipeline parallelism rather than tensor parallelism precisely because tensor sharding would multiply hop cost.

Concurrency falls out of the same mechanics. Because stages process different requests at once, maximum in-flight requests is roughly stages × micro-batch size, with the ceiling set by the KV cache that each wafer can hold for its own layer. Benchmark context is Kimi's own published results — 88.3% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and 91.2% on BrowseComp — so the Cerebras piece is not re-benchmarking the model, only showing it can be served without accuracy degradation at 4-bit. Two deliberate omissions are worth flagging: the post does not disclose per-wafer utilisation, and it does not say whether any Cerebras software stack is open source, so a comparison against a GPU reference implementation is still a reader's exercise. The 4-bit quantisation itself follows the Hugging Face release of Kimi K3, which keeps the result reproducible on paper.

For data engineering teams the practical implication is narrower than the headline suggests. A 59-wafer serving replica is not a cost line most organisations will match, but the shape — pure pipeline, weights pinned, cheap per-hop interconnect — is a reference point for anyone building long-context inference pipelines on their own clusters, and the 2 µs hop budget is a usable test for whether a given interconnect can support a very deep pipeline. Where the post is softer is on prefill versus decode behaviour and on how retries, partial failures and stage drains are handled, which are the operational details that decide whether such a pipeline survives contact with real traffic.

### 🎯 Takeaway (Hợp)
If your team is planning long-context inference on a GPU cluster, read the Cerebras post as a reference for pipeline parallelism rather than a cost model, and use its 2 µs per-hop figure as the budget to beat on your own interconnect. Với các team đang theo dõi infra cho open-weight model như Kimi K3, bài này là nguồn công khai duy nhất về cách map một mô hình gần 3T parameters sang 59 wafer mà vẫn giữ 4-bit quant chuẩn Hugging Face. Treat the throughput range as the headline it is: without per-wafer utilisation and queue behaviour, the "1000–2000+ tokens per second" number is a ceiling for a single pipeline, not a sustained SLO.

### 🔭 Overview (Tổng)
On 23 October 2026 ClickHouse published a customer piece on Shipsidekick's weekend migration off Neon to ClickHouse Managed Postgres, quoting founder and CEO Sawyer Bateman at length. The post is useful less as a vendor pitch than as a documented operational pattern for a workload that most data engineering teams hit at some point — a Postgres-backed SaaS whose connection count has outgrown its serverless tier. ClickPipes replicated about 500 GB in four to five hours, the team cut Neon's constant 100% CPU to a typical 5–10%, and a one-month shipment query that took one minute twenty seconds on Neon now returns in six seconds on the new instance. For teams running Postgres behind a Vercel/Next.js stack, the connection-pool rework is the piece worth reading closely.

### 🔬 Details (Phân)
The failure mode on Neon was the recurring Prisma × Vercel × PgBouncer trap. Prisma held pooled connections through Vercel's Fluid compute, Neon's PgBouncer sat on the other side, and connection pools kept exhausting until the database tripped around 3,000 connections and queries started timing out. On ClickHouse Managed Postgres, Shipsidekick removed the Vercel-side pool entirely and connects directly to the bundled PgBouncer that ships with each server; the resulting steady-state concurrency against Postgres fell from "around 1" active (with the rest of the fleet queueing) to "around 200" directly against the database, and cache hit rate rose from 30–35% to 99% because the server has 128 GiB of RAM rather than Neon's compute-unit ceiling. The sizing itself is specific: a 16 vCPU / 128 GiB / 3.7 TB storage-optimised instance with one standby, replicated into using ClickPipes over "4–5 hours" instead of Sai Srirampur's 1.5-day estimate.

One migration detail is worth lifting into a runbook. A partial unique index on the invoices table conflicted with mid-transaction replication; the fix was to drop the index, let ClickPipes drain, then recreate it on the destination. That is a pattern worth copying for any Postgres replication path, not just ClickHouse's, because partial uniques are the single most common CDC failure. The post also notes two secondary workloads that moved onto the primary: AI agents and MCP servers were given a read-only role, which initially let a runaway agent query push CPU to 40–50% until the team added statement and transaction timeouts and planned a dedicated read replica. Pricing is left half-stated — the post cites Neon's $2,300/month and PlanetScale's $4,000–5,000 estimates but calls the ClickHouse figure only "well under" the latter, so direct cost comparison still requires a quote.

Architecturally, the piece lands at an interesting moment. ClickHouse Managed Postgres is positioned as a destination for workloads where connection scalability and warm cache matter more than the serverless autoscale pattern that Neon pioneered, and the Shipsidekick numbers are the most concrete public datapoint so far for the Managed Postgres offering. The 10x headline is averaged across a mixed workload rather than a specific benchmark, which is honest but means teams should verify on their own query shapes before assuming a comparable speedup; and the data volume here (500 GB) is small enough that most of the win comes from cache efficiency rather than raw CPU, so a 10 TB workload is still an open question.

### 🎯 Takeaway (Hợp)
If you run a Prisma × Vercel × Postgres stack and your connection count is drifting toward the thousands, the Shipsidekick runbook is the right starting point — remove the application-side pool, lean on PgBouncer bundled with the destination, and set statement and transaction timeouts before letting any AI agent near the primary. Với các team đang quan sát ClickHouse Managed Postgres như một đích di trú, con số 500 GB và cache hit từ 30% lên 99% là datapoint cụ thể nhất tới hiện tại, nhưng đừng ngoại suy sang workload 10 TB mà chưa test. ### 🔭 Overview (Tổng)
On 24 October 2026 Tyler August at Hackaday wrote up a demo by nktbuilds that pushes a bog-standard coin-cell Bluetooth tracker tag to roughly 12 kilometres of one-way range, using no extra transmit power and no amplifier on the tag side. All of the gain lives on the receiver: a parabolic dish and a receiver with a much lower noise floor than a phone's built-in Bluetooth stack. For an IoT engineer the useful content is less about replacing a phone with a radio telescope and more about the way the link budget decomposes, because the standard mental model for "Bluetooth range" lumps TX and RX together in a way that this experiment deliberately breaks.

### 🔬 Details (Phân)
The Hackaday piece has a small arithmetic mismatch — the headline says 12 km while the body gives 1200 m in one sentence — and a reader comment flags it. The practical number to carry forward is the headline 12 km / ~7.5 mi figure that the write-up pins to the source video and the comparison with LoRa, which is where the article lands as a practical recommendation. The article names no chipset, no antenna gain figure and no receiver sensitivity number, which is unusual for a Hackaday post and means the specific link budget is not reproducible from the article alone. What is reproducible is the architecture: an unmodified transmitter tag, a dish-plus-sensitive-receiver on the ground, and a one-way link where "connected" effectively means "detectable", not "paired for data".

Two reader threads on the post are worth flagging because they constrain how the result generalises. The first is that the setup appears to be receive-only — the writer reports being able to see the beacon from the dish, not to pair and exchange data at distance. The second is that Bluetooth Classic's timing budget caps two-way links at roughly 1.5 km regardless of antenna gain, because the ACK round-trip has to fit inside protocol timers that the Classic specification sets. Those two threads together explain why the article closes with a straightforward "use LoRa for anything you want to actually communicate" conclusion rather than suggesting a new asset-tracking topology, and they are the pieces an engineer would want on paper before trying to reproduce the demo.

The quieter takeaway is the one about assumption-breaking in RF. Range figures printed on Bluetooth datasheets assume a symmetric link — phone-grade TX, phone-grade RX — and this experiment holds TX constant while taking RX as far as it will go. The resulting number tells you about asymmetric link design, which matters for beacons, lost-item tags, and some wildlife-tracking applications where the device side has to be cheap and tiny but the receiver can afford a dish and a cold-ish front end. The practical ceiling for a two-way product is still what the protocol timers allow, and that is where the LoRa recommendation stops being rhetorical.

### 🎯 Takeaway (Hợp)
Use this as a mental model for asymmetric Bluetooth link design, not as the basis for a product plan — a 12 km one-way detectable beacon is a reasonable upper bound, but any product that needs ACK-based data falls back onto Bluetooth Classic's roughly 1.5 km timing-budget ceiling. Với team đang chọn RF layer cho beacon tracker, con số 12 km chỉ áp dụng cho receive-only setup có parabolic dish; nếu cần hai chiều ở distance, LoRa vẫn là mặc định đúng. Treat the arithmetic mismatch between the headline (12 km) and the "1200 m" line in the body as a known erratum; the headline is the number the write-up and video defend.
